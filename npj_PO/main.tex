\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}
Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) is a biologically and clinically heterogeneous hematologic malignancy characterized by a broad range of genetic mutations, epigenetic alterations, and molecular subtypes \cite{cancer2013genomic,papaemmanuil2016genomic,kurzer2023updates,tyner2018functional}. According to the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program, AML is expected to account for an estimated $22{,}010$ new cases in the United States in 2025, representing approximately $1.1\%$ of all new cancer diagnoses and causing around $11{,}090$ deaths ($2\%$ of all cancer-related fatalities), underscoring its disproportionately high mortality relative to incidence. This clinical burden reflects the urgent need for improved therapeutic strategies and more precise treatment selection. The disease's complexity and variability present significant challenges to conventional treatment approaches, as patients often show diverse responses to standard chemotherapies and targeted agents. Despite progress in drug development, predicting which therapy will be most effective for a given patient remains a formidable clinical challenge \cite{tyner2018functional,yu2020advances}. While recent advances in genomics have led to the development of targeted therapies, accurately predicting individual AML patient response to specific drugs remains a major obstacle \cite{shafiei2025omics}.

In response to the persistent challenge of therapeutic heterogeneity in AML, precision medicine initiatives have gained momentum as a means to tailor treatments based on the unique molecular profiles of individual patients \cite{lohse2018precision}. One of the most significant efforts in this domain is the BeatAML project \cite{tyner2018functional}, a large-scale, multi-institutional initiative led by the Leukemia \& Lymphoma Society. The project emerged from the recognition that traditional one-size-fits-all treatment paradigms are insufficient for a disease as biologically diverse as AML \cite{bottomly2022integrative}. Earlier studies had shown that AML comprises multiple subtypes with distinct genomic and transcriptomic signatures, each of which may respond differently to specific therapies \cite{mo2023integrative,bottomly2022integrative}.

The BeatAML initiative \cite{bottomly2022integrative} sought to address this gap by building a comprehensive, high-resolution resource that integrates multi-omics data including: whole-exome sequencing, RNA sequencing, and clinical annotations together with \textit{ex vivo} drug sensitivity profiling. By systematically collecting and analyzing $942$ patient-derived AML specimens from $805$ individuals, the cohort captures a broad range of disease subtypes and stages. These samples were processed in two sequential tranches (Waves 1+2 and Waves 3+4), enabling longitudinal data generation and iterative refinement of analytical models. The study uncovered important cross-cohort associations, revealing that drug sensitivity is modulated not only by specific genetic mutations but also by the transcriptional state and differentiation trajectory of leukemic cells. However, despite these compelling insights, the study \cite{bottomly2022integrative} had limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item It relied on retrospective association analyses, limiting predictive inference and generalizability.
    \item Crucially, the analysis lacked predictive modeling capabilities, hindering its ability to forecast drug response in unseen patients.
    \item The study lacked the ability to identify the most sensitive or resistant patient strata for a novel drug.
    \item Drug response validation was confined to a single cohort, leaving the cross-population generalizability of identified response patterns untested.
\end{itemize}
Several machine learning approaches have been proposed to predict drug response in AML from multi-omics data. Karathanasis et al.\ \cite{karathanasis2024machine} combined clinical and RNA-seq features with ElasticNet regression, achieving $r_{pc} = 0.36$ across 122 BeatAML drugs. However, the linear architecture limits its capacity to efficiently capture non-linear patient-drug interactions. The MDREAM framework \cite{trac2023prediction} achieved $r_{sc} = 0.68$ using drug-specific ensemble SVM models, but trains an independent model per drug-fundamentally precluding generalization to novel therapeutics. Moreover, it restricts the evaluation to drugs with over $100$ patient–drug pairs, inflating apparent performance. The PT-AML framework \cite{jani2024pt}, benchmarked diverse ML architectures on the BeatAML cohort but relied on random cross-validation splits, significantly overestimating generalization performance for new patients. Most recently, NetAML \cite{wang2025netaml} constructed 87 drug-specific network-driven models using RNA-seq alone, but its reliance on a single omics modality, absence of drug molecular encoding, random train-test splits, and one-model-per-drug design collectively limit its multi-omics integration, chemical generalizability, and evaluation of performance on unseen patients.

To overcome these gaps, our work focuses on leveraging the rich multi-omics and clinical data from the BeatAML cohort to develop machine learning models \cite{lecun2015deep} which provide personalized treatment recommendations for AML patients referred as \textbf{PREDICT-AML}. PREDICT-AML accurately forecasts drug response for individual patients by integrating multi-modal data (transcriptomic, genomic, clinical, and drug mechanism-of-action features). The key characteristics of the PREDICT-AML framework are: (i) a transformer-based tabular model (TabPFN \cite{hollmann2023tabpfn}) as the primary predictive architecture; (ii) multiple state-of-the-art drug molecular encodings including Knowledge-Distilled embeddings (KD-Embed) \cite{mall2021modeling}, Chemical Language Models such as ChemBERTa \cite{chithrananda2020chemberta} and MolFormer \cite{ross2022large} based embeddings; all representations are SMILES sequence-based, deliberately excluding 3D conformer-dependent and explicit molecular graph encodings, which incur substantially higher preprocessing overhead and software dependencies without consistent predictive advantage in tabular drug sensitivity settings; (iii) three complementary cross-validation strategies to rigorously assess generalization across random, patient-stratified, and drug-stratified splits; and (iv) external validation on two independent AML cohorts - LeeAML \cite{lee2018machine} and FIMM-AML \cite{malani2022implementing} - that employ distinct drug sensitivity platforms and patient populations. (v) Comprehensive benchmarking of predictive models using standard machine learning as well as advanced deep learning models. The PREDICT-AML framework pursues the following core objectives:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Predictive Accuracy:} Accurately determine the most responsive drug for a patient using their unique multi-omics profile.
    \item \textbf{Model Interpretability:} Identify which molecular features (e.g., mutations, gene expression signatures, pathway and cellular enrichment) are most predictive of drug sensitivity or resistance, and derive calibrated per-prediction top features based on Shapley values \cite{shapley1953stochastic}.
    %confidence intervals natively from optimal TabPFN's approximate Bayesian posterior predictive distribution at no additional computational cost.
    \item \textbf{Translational Relevance:} Translate \textit{ex vivo} drug sensitivity predictions into patient-specific treatment prioritization, including repurposed agents, acknowledging that prospective \textit{in vivo} validation is necessary before clinical deployment.
    \item \textbf{External Generalizability:} Validate performance on independent cohorts spanning different populations, institutions, and drug sensitivity measurement platforms.
\end{itemize}

Ultimately, by building and validating PREDICT-AML across multiple cohorts, we seek to bridge the gap between association-based analysis and actionable, patient-specific treatment recommendations.

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{sec2}

\subsection{BeatAML Data Collection and Processing}\label{sec2.1}
We utilized data from the BeatAML cohort \cite{bottomly2022integrative}. The cohort is divided into two primary groups: Waves 1+2 and Waves 3+4. Waves 1+2 includes 649 specimens from 526 patients, while Waves 3+4 comprises 293 specimens from 279 patients, resulting in a total of 942 samples from 805 patients. For our analysis, we focused on $520$ samples for which mutation, transcriptomic, drug response, and clinical data were all available: $337$ samples from Waves 1+2 forming our training set and $183$ from Waves 3+4 forming our held-out test set. Clinical data included age, sex, disease status (alive/dead), and traits such as relapse, de novo AML, transformed AML, and prior malignancies. Patient ages ranged from 1 to 88 years, with 215 female and 305 male patients.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{LeeAML External Validation Cohort}\label{sec2.2}
The LeeAML cohort \cite{lee2018machine} is an independent AML patient dataset collected at the University of Washington and Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center comprising $30$ patients with ex vivo drug sensitivity profiling. Drug response was quantified as the Area Under the dose-response Curve (AUC) for $159$ inhibitors spanning diverse mechanistic classes. Clinical annotations include age, sex, European LeukemiaNet (ELN) cytogenetic risk classification (Döhner 2010 criteria), blast percentage, WBC count, and detailed treatment history. RNA-seq gene expression data was available for all patients, enabling application of the same transcriptomic feature engineering pipeline developed for BeatAML. For cross-cohort analysis, we identified $49$ drugs common between BeatAML and LeeAML cohorts. We then applied the BeatAML-trained PREDICT-AML model (on a common feature set) directly to LeeAML samples using these overlapping compounds. The evaluation set comprises $1{,}470$ patient-drug pairs in total.


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{FIMM-AML External Validation Cohort}\label{sec2.3}
The FIMM-AML cohort \cite{malani2022implementing} comprises $187$ AML patient samples from the Finnish Institute for Molecular Medicine (FIMM) in Helsinki, Finland, assembled through a functional precision medicine tumor board program. Drug sensitivity was measured using the Drug Sensitivity Score (DSS) \cite{malani2022implementing} for $540$ compounds across patient-derived samples. DSS is a normalized, dose-response-curve-integrated metric that accounts for drug selectivity and sensitivity, and differs in scale from the AUC metric used in BeatAML and LeeAML. Clinical annotations include age, sex, ELN 2017 risk classification, mutation status (FLT3, NPM1, KIT, CEBPA, and others), karyotype, and clinical outcomes including overall survival and event-free survival. RNA-seq data was available for all samples. For cross-cohort integration, we identified $78$ drugs common between BeatAML and FIMM-AML. To bridge the two drug sensitivity platforms, we trained the PREDICT-AML model on the full BeatAML training set (on common feature set) and evaluated predictions on FIMM-AML using the common drugs, reporting both AUC-scale predictions and Spearman correlation against DSS scores. After inner-joining patients with complete multi-omics feature data, the evaluation set comprises $8{,}357$ patient-drug pairs in total for the FIMM-AML external cohort.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{RNA-Seq and DNA-Seq Analysis}\label{sec2.4}
We processed bulk RNA-seq data from BeatAML Waves 1+2 and Waves 3+4 as training and test sets, respectively to extract features, and applied the same pipeline to both external cohorts. For the BeatAML cohort, we performed patient segmentation based on their gene expression profiles to assess data quality and identify underlying transcriptional clusters.

We applied t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) \cite{van2008visualizing} on the transcriptomic profiles of BeatAML patients across Waves 1+2 (training) and Waves 3+4 (test). The resulting t-SNE plot (Supplementary Fig.~1A) displayed a uniform distribution of samples with no visible segregation between training and test sets, confirming effective normalization and quality control. Next, we evaluated the impact of different sets of highly variable genes on clustering robustness. Using the silhouette criterion \cite{rousseeuw1987silhouettes} across a range of 100 to 1,000 most variable genes, clustering performance peaked at $150$ variable genes (Supplementary Fig.~1B). We then assessed the optimal number of clusters over $k \in [2, 10]$, which revealed that three clusters ($k=3$) provided the most stable grouping (Supplementary Fig.~1C), confirmed by PCA visualization (Supplementary Fig.~1D).

\textbf{Most Variable Genes}: The $150$ most variable and 736 oncogenes \cite{sondka2018cosmic} (with 9 overlapping genes) were scaled and used as gene expression features.

\textbf{Cell State and Module Enrichment}: Cell state gene sets (cDC-like, GMP-like, HSC-like, Monocyte-like, Progenitor-like, Promono-like) and Module gene sets (M1-M14) were obtained from the BeatAML study \cite{bottomly2022integrative}. We then performed single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) using the GSVA (v1.50.5) package in R (v4.4.1) to estimate cell state and module enrichments. The un-normalized cell state enrichment scores varied between $[0,1]$, while the normalized module enrichment scores were in $[-0.5,0.5]$.

\textbf{Pathway Activity}: A total of 54 oncogenic pathways (gene sets) were obtained from \cite{roelands2020oncogenic}. We used the ssGSEA technique to estimate oncogenic pathway activity \cite{roelands2020oncogenic, mall2021network}. The normalized pathway activity scores were in $[-0.5,0.5]$.

\subsection{Drug Data and Multi-Modal Fingerprint Generation}\label{sec2.5}
We collected drug response data from the BeatAML dataset. A total of $165$ unique drugs were available, of which $150$ were represented in the training set (Waves~1+2) and $153$ in the held-out test set (Waves~3+4); $14$ drugs appeared exclusively in the test set, enabling assessment of generalization to unseen compounds. After intersecting with available drug feature representations, $150$ test drugs were included in the final evaluation. We derived four complementary molecular representations for each drug:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Physico-Chemical Feature Representation (PC):} Drug molecular features were derived using RDKit \cite{bento2020open}: $148$ non-redundant and non-correlated molecular descriptors, $30$ graph-theoretic features computed via NetworkX (v3.5), Morgan circular fingerprints (radius~3, 128~bits), and MACCS structural keys (167~bits, first bit inactive). This 473-dimensional representation was recently identified as optimal for predicting diverse molecular properties \cite{harod2025effichem}; the full feature list is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

    \item \textbf{Knowledge-Distilled Embeddings (KD-Embed):} Fixed-length Knowledge Distilled (KD) latent vectors were produced by a Teacher Forcing Long Short-Term Memory (TF-LSTM) sequence-to-sequence autoencoder \cite{mall2021modeling} trained on $\approx\!2.5$ million compounds from PubChem \cite{kim2025pubchem} and the MOSES benchmark \cite{10.3389/fphar.2020.565644} (see Figure \ref{fig:fig_lstm}). Overall, $96.7\%$ of reconstructed compounds were valid as determined by RDKit, with mean categorical cross-entropy of $0.001$.
    
    \item \textbf{ChemBERTa Embeddings:} We encoded drug SMILES strings using ChemBERTa \cite{chithrananda2020chemberta}, a transformer pre-trained on 77~million compounds from PubChem using masked language modeling, producing $384$-dimensional contextual representations that capture chemical substructure and neighbourhood information. Embeddings were extracted zero-shot, without fine-tuning the ChemBERTa model.
    \item \textbf{MolFormer Embeddings:} We generated $768$-dimensional embeddings using MolFormer \cite{ross2022large}, a large-scale rotary-position transformer pre-trained on $\sim\!1.1$ billion SMILES strings from PubChem and ZINC. MolFormer was designed to capture long-range molecular dependencies and global structural properties. As with ChemBERTa, embeddings were extracted in zero-shot fashion.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Drug Pathway Proximity Estimation}\label{sec2.6}
To estimate a computational proximity of each drug to individual oncogenic pathway in a patient-specific manner, we exploit drug target information available from DrugBank \cite{knox2024drugbank}. Drug targets (genes/proteins) serve as starting points in the human protein-protein interaction (PPI) network obtained from the STRING database, retaining only interactions with strong experimental evidence (score $\ge 700$).

Starting from drug targets, network propagation via a random-walk with restart (RWR) approach \cite{sherif2022immune,mall2013kernel} produces a propagation vector representing the global proximity of each protein to a given drug. To obtain a patient-specific local propagation vector, we weight each protein's propagation score by the corresponding gene expression from RNA-seq. The resulting vector reflects how ``close'' an oncogenic pathway is to a given drug in the molecular context of a specific patient: if pathway member genes rank highly in the sorted propagation vector, the drug has close proximity to that pathway, and vice versa. Pathway proximity was estimated using the AUCell package (v1.24.0) \cite{aibar2017scenic}, computing the AUC, where higher AUC corresponds to closer drug-pathway proximity for a given patient (see Fig \ref{fig:fig_drugpathway_auc}).

\subsection{Training and Test Data Preparation}\label{sec2.7}
Using the approach described above, we assembled comprehensive patient representations comprising: mutation profiles (frequency and mutation type for top mutated genes), gene expression profiles (150 most variable genes and oncogenes), and enrichment scores (pathway activity, module enrichment, cell state). Drug representations were one of the four molecular encodings (PC, KD-Embed, ChemBERTa, MolFormer) supplemented by drug target annotations from DrugBank \cite{knox2024drugbank} and patient-specific unique drug-pathway AUC proximity scores.

The merged BeatAML training and held-out test datasets were constructed by performing inner joins between the drug response table, patient feature table (keyed by patient ID), and drug feature table (keyed by compound ID) using the \texttt{merge} function in \texttt{pandas}. The resulting training set contains $33{,}803$ patient-drug records and the test set contains $18{,}826$ records. Feature dimensionality ranges from $277$ to $2,034$ depending on the drug representation used and the patient feature group ablation configuration. An analogous pipeline was applied to LeeAML and FIMM-AML, generating merged drug-patient feature matrices for external evaluation. The overall PREDICT-AML workflow is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fig3}.

\subsection{Machine Learning Models}\label{sec2.8}
We developed and trained diverse machine learning models to predict drug response using molecular, genomic, and clinical features together with drug representations.

\textbf{Standard models} included generalized linear regression (GLR) \cite{nelder1972generalized}, support vector regression (SVR) \cite{chandorkar2015fixed}, Random Forests (RF) \cite{breiman2001random}, XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm} and CatBoost \cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost}. \textbf{Deep learning models} processing the SMILES string directly included convolutional neural networks (CNN) \cite{sermanet2012convolutional}, LSTM \cite{gers2000learning}, graph attention networks (GAT) \cite{velivckovic2017graph}, and graph convolutional networks (GCN) \cite{kipf2017semi}.

\textbf{TabPFN} \cite{hollmann2023tabpfn} serves as the primary model in PREDICT-AML. TabPFN (Tabular Prior-data Fitted Networks) is a transformer-based model that treats tabular datasets as contexts and generates predictions in a single forward pass without gradient-based optimization at inference time. It is pre-trained via meta-learning on a large collection of synthetic datasets sampled from a structured Bayesian prior, allowing it to perform in-context learning and naturally handle the heterogeneous, high-dimensional, mixed-type feature matrices characteristic of multi-omics drug response data. Hyperparameters for all tree-based, TabPFN and deep learning models were optimized using Optuna \cite{akiba2019optuna} with 5-fold cross-validation on the BeatAML training set.

Performance metrics included mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), Pearson correlation ($r_{pc}$), Spearman correlation ($r_{sc}$), and coefficient of determination (R$^2$). Lower MAE and RMSE and higher $r_{pc}$, $r_{sc}$, and R$^2$ indicate better performance.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Cross-Validation Strategies}\label{sec2.9}
To rigorously assess different aspects of model generalizability, we evaluated all models under three distinct 5-fold cross-validation (CV) strategies on BeatAML training set:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Random CV:} Patient-drug pairs are assigned randomly to folds. This standard strategy tests interpolation within the observed distribution of patients and drugs.
    \item \textbf{Patient-stratified CV (PS-CV):} Folds are partitioned by patient identity, ensuring no patient appears in both training and validation. This evaluates the model's ability to predict drug response for new, previously unseen patients - the most clinically relevant scenario.
    \item \textbf{Drug-stratified CV (DS-CV):} Folds are partitioned by drug identity, ensuring no drug appears in both training and validation. This evaluates generalization to structurally or mechanistically novel drugs not encountered during training.
\end{enumerate}

Each configuration is independently evaluated with all four drug molecular representations (PC, KD-Embed, ChemBERTa, MolFormer), yielding 12 experimental conditions for any modelling technique including the primary TabPFN model.

\section{Results}\label{sec3}

\subsection{Benchmarking Machine Learning Models on BeatAML}\label{sec3.1}
We benchmarked the full suite of classical machine learning and deep learning models on the BeatAML training (Waves 1+2) and test (Waves 3+4) sets. \textbf{Patient-stratified CV is the primary benchmark.} In PS-CV, each fold withholds an entire subset of patients from training, directly simulating the clinical scenario in which a model must generalize to new individuals not seen during development. Under PS-CV, all four drug representations achieve consistent performance: $r_{sc} \approx 0.706$-$0.719$ (RMSE $\approx 42.7$-$43.5$). The top-performing configuration is KD-Embed ($r_{sc} = 0.719 \pm 0.011$, RMSE $= 42.687 \pm 1.458$; MolFormer performs comparably at $r_{sc} = 0.708 \pm 0.019$). Crucially, the held-out test performance ($r_{sc} = 0.680$-$0.685$, RMSE $= 51.3$-$51.6$) closely tracks the PS-CV estimate, with a mean generalization gap of $\%\Delta r_{sc} \approx 4.0\%$ as indicated in Table \ref{table:tabpfn_cv}, confirming that PS-CV is a reliable and honest predictor of real-world performance on unseen patients.

\textbf{Random CV overestimates generalization.} A critical observation from Table~\ref{table:tabpfn_cv} is that random CV yields $r_{sc} \approx 0.796$-$0.800$ (RMSE $\approx 36.4$-$36.7$) for all drug representations - a Spearman correlation approximately $12\%$-$13\%$ higher than the PS-CV estimate. Yet the held-out test performance under these same configurations ($r_{sc} = 0.676$-$0.684$, RMSE $= 51.3$-$51.9$) is nearly identical to the PS-CV test performance, exposing a $\%\Delta r_{sc}$ of $14.5$-$15.5\%$ between CV and test. This inflation arises because random fold assignment places different drug-patient pairs from the same patients in both training and validation, allowing the model to implicitly interpolate between known patient states rather than extrapolating to genuinely new individuals. These results underscore that random CV is an inappropriate benchmark for evaluation. Full random CV results for all 11 models are in Supplementary Table~S5.

\textbf{Drug-stratified CV reveals chemical generalization limits.} Under DS-CV, CV performance drops substantially to $r_{sc} \approx 0.268$-$0.348$ (RMSE $\approx 57.4$-$59.2$), appearing to suggest severe underperformance. However, the held-out test $r_{sc}$ under DS-CV-tuned configurations ($0.662$-$0.681$) is comparable to the PS-CV test $r_{sc}$, yielding large negative $\%\Delta r_{sc}$ values ($-95.4$ to $-147.0\%$). This paradox arises because the held-out Waves~3+4 test set shares most drugs with the training set, so test performance is not drug-stratified and benefits from drug familiarity absent during DS-CV folds. Nonetheless, DS-CV correctly identifies chemical language models as advantageous for novel-drug generalization: ChemBERTa ($r_{sc} = 0.348 \pm 0.096$) and MolFormer ($r_{sc} = 0.346 \pm 0.030$) outperform physicochemical-only representations ($r_{sc} = 0.268 \pm 0.042$) in the CV folds, a signal that does not emerge under PS-CV or random CV where patient features dominate. Full DS-CV results for all 11 models are in Supplementary Table~S4.

Figure~\ref{fig:tabpfn_scatter} shows predicted versus observed AUC values for the best-performing TabPFN configuration (all patient feature groups, KD-Embed) on the full held-out test set and a subset of 14 novel drugs absent from training, illustrating patient-level generalization and zero-shot drug generalization respectively. \subsection{Ablation Study on Feature Group Contributions}\label{sec3.3}
We performed a systematic ablation study of the optimal PREDICT-AML model (i.e. TabPFN with KD-Embed) on BeatAML dataset to quantify the contribution of individual patient feature groups to TabPFN prediction performance. Feature groups were: (i)~Gene Expression (150 most variable genes + oncogenes); (ii)~Mutation profiles; (iii)~Pathway enrichment scores; (iv)~Cell state and module enrichment (Clinical, CellType, Module). These were evaluated individually and in pairwise and triple combinations with two possible drug representations (PC and KD-Embed). Baseline models using all features (all four groups combined) served as references as shown in Supp. Table S3.

Systematic ablation as depicted in Supp. Table S3 reveals that gene expression features are the single strongest contributor to drug sensitivity prediction. Using Gene Expression alone with KD-Embed drug representation achieves test $r_{sc} = 0.677$ ($r_{pc} = 0.682$), recovering $98.8\%$ of the full four-group baseline performance ($r_{sc} = 0.685$, $r_{pc} = 0.690$). Mutation profiles provide the weakest isolated signal ($r_{sc} = 0.646$, $r_{pc} = 0.647$), while Pathway enrichment ($r_{sc} = 0.657$) and Clinical/CellType/Module features ($r_{sc} = 0.658$) offer intermediate contributions. Pairwise combinations consistently improve on their constituent single-group models: Gene Expression + Mutation achieves $r_{sc} = 0.680$ ($r_{pc} = 0.685$), and notably, Clinical/CellType/Module combined with Mutation alone matches full-model performance ($r_{sc} = 0.685$, $r_{pc} = 0.689$) despite excluding the Gene Expression and Pathway groups - reflecting complementary signals between cell-state transcriptional context and somatic mutational load. Among triple combinations, Clinical/CellType/Module + Gene Expression + Mutation achieves $r_{sc} = 0.684$ ($r_{pc} = 0.689$), with the full four-group model providing only marginal additional gain ($\Delta r_{sc} \leq 0.001$). These findings are consistent across both PC and KD-Embed drug representations (Supplementary Table~S3 and Supp. Figure S5), confirming that feature group contributions are stable with respect to drug molecular representation choice, and that the four patient feature groups together capture the full multi-omics signal with modest redundancy at the margins.

\subsection{Model Interpretability driven by SHAP}\label{sec3.4}
To interpret the predictions of PREDICT-AML (TabPFN with KD-Embed) model, we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)~\cite{lundberg2017unified}, a game-theoretic post-hoc explanation framework that decomposes each model output into additive per-feature contributions. For a given patient-drug pair $x$, SHAP expresses the prediction as:
\begin{equation}
    f(x) \;=\; E[f(X)] \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i,
    \label{eq:shap}
\end{equation}
where $E[f(X)] = 197.3$ AUC units is the population-average baseline prediction over the BeatAML test set, and $\phi_i$ is the Shapley value of feature $i$, the average marginal contribution of feature $i$ across all possible subsets of the remaining features~\cite{shapley1953stochastic}. Positive $\phi_i > 0$ pushes the prediction toward higher AUC (resistance); $\phi_i < 0$ pushes it toward lower AUC (sensitivity). Global feature importance derived from SHAP across test-set predictions is summarized in Supplementary Figure~S6, which shows that 23 of the top 30 features by mean $|\phi_i|$ are KD-Embed latent-space dimensions (\texttt{LS\_*}), confirming that drug molecular identity is the primary driver of predicted response at the population level. Additional patient-derived transcriptomic features including \textit{DNTT}, \textit{CSPG4}, \textit{CEACAM6}, and \textit{CDC42BPA} expression provide secondary patient-specific modulation.

Figures~\ref{fig:shap_waterfall_resistant} and~\ref{fig:shap_waterfall_sensitive} present individual-level SHAP waterfall plots for four representative test-set patient-drug pairs: two resistant examples (Figure~\ref{fig:shap_waterfall_resistant}) and two sensitive examples (Figure~\ref{fig:shap_waterfall_sensitive}). In each panel, bars accumulate from the population baseline $E[f(X)]$ to the individual prediction $f(x)$; red arrows indicate positive SHAP contributions (toward resistance) and blue arrows indicate negative contributions (toward sensitivity).

\subsection{Stratified Performance Profiles and Multi-omics Drivers of Predicted Drug Sensitivity}\label{sec3.5}

To characterize how our optimized TabPFN model (PREDICT-AML) performs at the resolution of individual patients and individual drugs, and to identify the patient-level multi-omics features that underpin predicted drug sensitivity, we stratified test-set predictions across the $183$ BeatAML test patients and $117$ drugs (each drug with patient response for over 100 patients) and performed systematic feature association analyses for the top-$10$ best-predicted drugs.

\noindent\textbf{Per-patient and per-drug predictive performance.} Figure~\ref{fig:perf_patient_drug}A shows the distribution of per-patient Pearson correlations ($r_{pc}$) computed by correlating PREDICT-AML predictions against observed AUC across all drugs tested for each individual. The model achieves a mean $r_{pc} = 0.746 \pm 0.145$ across the $183$ test patients, with $92.9\%$ of patients exceeding $r_{pc} > 0.5$ and $73.2\%$ exceeding $r_{pc} > 0.7$. The distribution is left-skewed with a small tail of harder-to-predict patients (minimum $r_{pc} = 0.092$), likely reflecting patients whose transcriptomic profiles deviate substantially from the training distribution. Figure~\ref{fig:perf_patient_drug}B shows the complementary per-drug perspective: correlating predictions against observed AUC tested with a given drug. Drug-level $r_{pc}$ is markedly more variable (mean $r_{pc} = 0.335 \pm 0.149$; range $0.15$-$0.82$), with only $15.4\%$ of drugs exceeding $r_{pc} > 0.5$. The top-predicted drugs include Venetoclax ($r_{pc} = 0.816$), Elesclomol ($r_{pc} = 0.630$), Rapamycin ($r_{pc} = 0.629$), Sorafenib ($r_{pc} = 0.610$), and Panobinostat ($r_{pc} = 0.571$). The stark contrast between high patient-level and moderate drug-level performance is mechanistically interpretable: patient multi-omics profiles capture broad transcriptional states that generalize across many drugs tested on the same individual, whereas drug-level predictability is additionally constrained by the pharmacological specificity of each compound and the extent to which patient features within the training set encode drug-relevant molecular variation.

\noindent\textbf{Multi-omics feature associations with top-drug predictions.}~To identify patient-level molecular determinants of predicted drug response, we fitted per-feature linear regression models ($\hat{y} \sim \beta_j x_j$) for each of the $\sim\!1{,}300$ non-latent-space patient features against predicted normalized AUC across all patients for each of the top-$10$ drugs, applying FDR correction (Benjamini-Hochberg $q < 0.05$, $R^2 > 0.15$). Figure~\ref{fig:perf_patient_drug}c shows the resulting $\beta$ matrix for features consistently significant across $\geq\!8$ of the $10$ drugs, sorted by mean $R^2$. Seven features were significant in all $10$ drugs: KRAS signaling pathway activity (\texttt{KRAS\_signaling\_up} and its AUC-transformed counterpart), \textit{BCL6} expression, \texttt{Monocyte-like} cell-state enrichment, module score \texttt{M6}, hypoxia/adenosine immune-cell suppression score, and phospholipase pathway activity. A further $16$ features were significant in $9$ of $10$ drugs, including \textit{MAFB} expression, \textit{CD300E} expression, \textit{FAS} expression, immunogenic cell death (ICD) pathway score, p38 MAPK signaling, and \textit{AKT2} expression, collectively spanning proliferative signalling, monocytic differentiation, and immune-stress axes. Crucially, the directionality of these associations is drug-class-specific: \texttt{Monocyte-like} enrichment carries a strongly positive $\beta$ for Venetoclax ($\hat{\beta} = 0.77$, $R^2 = 0.87$), indicating that monocyte-like AML patients are predicted to be Venetoclax-resistant, while the same feature carries a negative $\beta$ for MEK inhibitors Trametinib ($\hat{\beta} = -0.47$) and Selumetinib ($\hat{\beta} = -0.41$) and for Panobinostat ($\hat{\beta} = -0.54$, $R^2 = 0.80$), indicating sensitivity. KRAS signaling likewise flips sign: positive for Venetoclax ($\hat{\beta} = 6.41$) yet negative for Elesclomol ($\hat{\beta} = -1.95$) and all MEK inhibitors, reflecting the opposing pharmacological axes of BCL-2 versus kinase-directed therapies.

\noindent\textbf{Known and novel biological insights.} Several of the identified associations recapitulate established AML biology. Venetoclax resistance in monocyte-differentiated AML is well-documented: monocytic blasts rely on MCL-1 rather than BCL-2 for survival, and multiple independent studies confirm that a monocytic transcriptional state - driven in part by RAS pathway activation - confers BCL-2 inhibitor failure~\cite{kennedy2026ras, yashar2026pu1, mohanty2024venetoclax, allen2025venetoclax}. Our finding that \textit{MAFB} expression ($R^2 = 0.72$ for Venetoclax) is among the strongest per-patient predictors of Venetoclax resistance is consistent with MAFB's established role as a master regulator of monocytic commitment~\cite{ventura2025mafb, yang2015dnmt3a}. Similarly, the negative association of \textit{CD300E} - a canonical surface marker of mature monocytes~\cite{li2023cd300e} - with MEK inhibitor predictions aligns with evidence that CCL2/CCR2-driven monocytic differentiation modulates MEK inhibitor response in AML~\cite{modak2024targeting}, and that KRAS allelic imbalance amplifies MAP kinase pathway dependence and MEK inhibitor sensitivity~\cite{burgess2017kras, pedicona2022lsd1}. \textit{BCL6} expression was significant in all $10$ drugs; while BCL6's role as a transcriptional repressor mediating resistance to FLT3 inhibitors~\cite{zavorka2021bcl6} and as a chemosensitizing target in AML~\cite{zhang2023bcl6} is established, its pan top-10 drug significance across mechanistically distinct compound classes - from BCL-2 inhibitors to mTOR inhibitors to multi-kinase inhibitors - is a novel observation that warrants further investigation. Finally, consistent significance of the ICD pathway score and \textit{HMGB1} signaling across $9$ of $10$ drugs is to our knowledge unreported in AML drug sensitivity modelling: ICD hallmarks (calreticulin surface exposure, HMGB1 release, ATP secretion) have been mechanistically linked to therapeutic response in multiple solid-tumour contexts, but their role as a transcriptional biomarker of AML drug sensitivity is novel and merits prospective validation.

The external validation confirms that PREDICT-AML generalizes to the independent AML cohort with distinct patient demographics, treatment protocols, and sample processing workflows.

\subsection{Heterogeneity in Drug Response}\label{sec3.7}
To characterize inter-patient variability in predicted drug sensitivity, we computed for each drug the mean and standard deviation (SD) of the PREDICT-AML predictions across all patients in each cohort. Figure~\ref{fig:external}(C-E) shows the resulting mean–SD scatter across 150 drugs (BeatAML), 78 drugs (FIMM-AML), and 49 drugs (LeeAML), with point colour encoding the mean predicted AUC.

A consistent inverse relationship between mean predicted AUC and inter-patient variability is evident across all three cohorts: drugs predicted to be broadly effective (low mean AUC, dark purple) cluster in the upper-left of each panel with high SD, whereas broadly resistant drugs (high mean AUC, yellow) concentrate in the lower-right with near-uniform predictions. From Figure \ref{fig:external}C, we observe that in BeatAML, the most heterogeneous predictions are for Venetoclax (mean AUC $= 149.5$, SD $= 53.2$), Panobinostat (mean AUC $= 105.3$, SD $= 37.6$), Selumetinib (mean AUC $= 204.7$, SD $= 34.2$), and Trametinib (mean AUC $= 138.5$, SD $= 30.8$), while drugs such as Vismodegib (SD $= 2.6$) and GDC-0879 (SD $= 2.8$) show nearly uniform pan-resistance predictions (mean AUC $\approx 277$-$278$). The same pattern is replicated in FIMM-AML, where Venetoclax (SD $= 28.5$) and Panobinostat (SD $= 15.9$) again rank as the most heterogeneous drugs, followed by Selumetinib (SD $= 11.5$), Palbociclib (SD $= 10.4$), and Trametinib (SD $= 10.1$) (see Figure \ref{fig:external}D). The LeeAML cohort displays a substantially narrower SD range (SD $= 1.3$-$4.9$; mean SD $= 2.4$) attributable to the smaller patient cohort ($n = 30$), yet retains the same drug-level ordering, with Panobinostat (SD $= 4.9$) and SNS-032 (SD $= 4.3$) leading the heterogeneity ranking as depicted in Figure \ref{fig:external}E. 

The cross-cohort consistency of Venetoclax and Panobinostat as outlier high-heterogeneity drugs is biologically meaningful: Venetoclax (BCL-2 inhibitor) is clinically known to be effective in AML subtypes harbouring IDH1/2 and NPM1 mutations but inactive in others~\cite{bisaillon2020genetic, diamantidis2025factors, zhang2020integrated}, and Panobinostat (pan-HDAC inhibitor) responses are governed by highly patient-specific epigenetic landscapes~\cite{babu2021jak, tang2025epigenetic, kuhn2025epigenetic}. Similarly, the recurrent heterogeneity of MEK inhibitors (Selumetinib, Trametinib) across BeatAML and FIMM-AML reflects differential RAS/MAPK pathway activation across patients~\cite{modak2024targeting, chen2025ras}. These results demonstrate that PREDICT-AML captures biologically grounded patient stratification signals. It is noteworthy that the clinical utility of model-guided selection is highest for drugs with large predicted inter-patient heterogeneity, such as Venetoclax and Panobinostat, where the model can identify the sensitive subpopulation and spare non-responders, whereas uniformly resistant drugs (e.g., Vismodegib, GDC-0879) are correctly flagged as poor candidates irrespective of patient multi-omics profile.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec4}
PREDICT-AML is a multi-omics driven machine learning framework for personalized AML drug response prediction that demonstrates robust generalization to unseen patients and independent external cohorts. The following discussion contextualizes the principal findings within the broader literature and highlights novel contributions.

\textbf{Patient-stratified CV as the clinical benchmark}: A methodologically important finding of this work is that standard random cross-validation substantially overestimates real-world predictive utility. Random CV inflates $r_{pc}$ by $\sim\!13\%$ relative to patient-stratified CV (PS-CV; $0.808$ vs.\ $0.716$ for TabPFN with MolFormer), because random fold assignment places different drug-patient pairs from the same individuals in both training and validation sets, permitting implicit interpolation across known patient states. PS-CV, which withholds entire patients from training, directly simulates the clinical deployment scenario and constitutes the appropriate evaluation standard. We therefore recommend that patient-stratified evaluation be adopted as the primary benchmark in future AML drug response prediction studies.

\textbf{TabPFN as optimal architecture}: TabPFN \cite{hollmann2023tabpfn} achieves the highest held-out test performance ($r_{pc} = 0.690$, MAE $= 37.712$) with a small CV-to-test generalization gap among all evaluated architectures ($\%\Delta r_{pc} = 4.7\%$), whereas deep learning models such as CNN and LSTM attain higher raw CV scores but degrade substantially at test time ($\%\Delta r_{pc} = 8$-$12\%$). This superiority in calibrated generalization is attributable to TabPFN's meta-learned in-context mechanism, which adapts to the test distribution at inference time without gradient updates, thereby mitigating patient-level distribution shift. For novel-drug generalization specifically, chemical language model representations (ChemBERTa, MolFormer) confer a systematic advantage over physicochemical descriptors, improving drug-stratified CV $r_{pc}$ from $0.306$ to $0.377$, and are therefore the preferred encoding when predicting responses to structurally unseen compounds.

\textbf{Multi-omics feature contributions and a novel reduced-panel insight}: Gene expression alone recovers $98.8\%$ of full-model performance, establishing transcriptomic state as the dominant predictor of pharmacological sensitivity. A novel and clinically significant observation is that combining somatic mutations with cell-state enrichment scores, in the absence of gene expression or pathway features, matches full four-group performance ($r_{sc} = 0.685$, $r_{pc} = 0.689$). This indicates that these two modalities encode largely orthogonal aspects of the drug sensitivity landscape: somatic mutations define subtype identity and direct oncogenic pathway dependencies, while cell-state enrichment scores capture differentiation-lineage commitments and immune microenvironmental context that mutation profiles alone cannot resolve. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that a parsimonious mutation-plus-cell-state feature panel can achieve full multi-omics parity in AML drug response prediction, with direct implications for resource-limited clinical settings where high-depth RNA-seq may be unavailable.

\textbf{Multi-cohort external validation}: PREDICT-AML was validated simultaneously on two independent external cohorts spanning different pharmacological measurement platforms (AUC vs.\ DSS), geographic sites (United States vs.\ Finland), and patient cohort sizes. LeeAML validation ($r_{pc} = 0.597$, $r_{sc} = 0.622$) confirms that transcriptomic and genomic signals predictive of drug sensitivity in BeatAML are transferable to patients from a geographically distinct clinical center. FIMM-AML validation ($|r_{pc}| = 0.557$) demonstrates cross-platform consistency despite systematic scale and directionality differences between AUC and DSS metrics, establishing that PREDICT-AML captures biologically generalizable sensitivity signals. %rather than dataset-specific correlates.

\textbf{SHAP interpretability and biological validation}: The SHAP decomposition reveals a biologically coherent two-tier prediction architecture: KD-Embed latent dimensions (23 of the top 30 features by mean $|\phi_i|$) encode drug molecular identity as the dominant population-level driver, while patient-derived features provide targeted individual-level modulation. Importantly, several patient features recovered by SHAP are independently supported by the AML biology literature, serving as internal validation of the model's learned representations. \textit{IDH2} mutation status opposes resistance ($\phi = -6.3$) in an otherwise strongly resistant patient, consistent with the established mechanism by which IDH1/2 mutations produce the oncometabolite R-2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), suppress cytochrome c oxidase activity, and create BCL-2 synthetic lethality~\cite{chan2015idh, bisaillon2020genetic}. That PREDICT-AML internalizes this axis without explicit pathway supervision validates the biological authenticity of its learned representations.

\textit{MAFB} expression exhibits bidirectional context-dependence ($\phi = -9.3$ toward sensitivity vs.\ $\phi = +7.7$ toward resistance across two patients), consistent with its documented dual myeloid role: MAFB drives pro-differentiation sensitivity in committed progenitors~\cite{ventura2025mafb}, whereas in M4/M5 monocytic AML it upregulates MCL1 and BCL2A1, conferring resistance to BH3 mimetics and Venetoclax~\cite{allen2025venetoclax}. The opposing SHAP signs across patients may thus reflect whether a given patient's MAFB expression state represents productive monocytic differentiation or pathological monocytic reprogramming. Notably, \textit{POU4F1} (BRN3A) emerges as the strongest patient-derived sensitivity feature ($\phi = -11.0$); its overexpression is a hallmark of t(8;21) AML, where the AML1/ETO fusion oncoprotein drives a leukemogenic transcriptional program~\cite{dunne2010pou4f1, fortier2010pou4f1}. This constitutes a novel evidence linking POU4F1 transcriptional state to ex~vivo drug sensitivity in AML, representing a candidate testable hypothesis. %Similarly, \textit{DEFA1} expression, a canonical marker of granulocytic maturation, opposing resistance ($\phi = -8.5$) in a strongly resistant patient is without precedent in the published AML drug response literature, identifying it as a candidate novel biomarker warranting prospective experimental validation.

\textbf{Top-10 drug multi-omics drivers of predicted response}: Per-feature linear regression across the top-10 predicted drugs (FDR $q < 0.05$, $R^2 > 0.15$) identified seven pan-drug determinants significant in all ten compounds, including KRAS signaling activity, \textit{BCL6} expression, monocyte-like cell-state enrichment, and an immunogenic cell death (ICD) pathway score. Directionality is drug-class-specific: the monocyte-like score strongly predicts Venetoclax resistance ($\hat{\beta} = 0.77$, $R^2 = 0.87$), consistent with MCL-1 dependence in monocytic blasts~\cite{kennedy2026ras, mohanty2024venetoclax, allen2025venetoclax}, yet associates with sensitivity to MEK inhibitors and Panobinostat ($\hat{\beta} = -0.54$, $R^2 = 0.80$)~\cite{pedicona2022lsd1, burgess2017kras}. Two findings are, to our knowledge, novel: \textit{BCL6} significance spanning BCL-2, mTOR, MEK, and multi-kinase inhibitors extends its established role in FLT3 inhibitor resistance~\cite{zavorka2021bcl6} to a broader pharmacological context; and consistent ICD pathway significance across nine of ten drugs identifies a transcriptional biomarker axis of AML drug sensitivity not previously reported.

\textbf{Drug heterogeneity and clinical patient stratification}: The cross-cohort drug heterogeneity analysis reveals a clinically actionable principle: PREDICT-AML demonstrates greatest clinical utility for drugs with high inter-patient variability in predicted response, precisely those for which one-size-fits-all treatment selection carries the greatest risk of harm. Venetoclax (SD $= 53.2$ AUC) and Panobinostat (SD $= 37.6$ AUC) are the highest-heterogeneity drugs across multiple cohorts, consistent with their well-established dependence on patient-specific molecular determinants i.e. IDH1/2 and NPM1 mutational status and monocytic differentiation state for Venetoclax~\cite{bisaillon2020genetic, zhang2020integrated, allen2025venetoclax}, and patient-specific chromatin compaction and HDAC isoform landscapes for Panobinostat~\cite{tang2025epigenetic, kuhn2025epigenetic}. By contrast, Vismodegib and GDC-0879 exhibit near-uniform pan-resistance (SD $< 3$ AUC) across all patients, indicating that model-guided selection provides no meaningful stratification benefit for these agents. %This binary stratification including high-heterogeneity agents amenable to patient selection versus uniformly resistant agents amenable to de-prioritization constitutes a novel clinical triage framework emerging from the multi-cohort analysis.

%\textbf{Prediction uncertainty}: Calibrated $95\%$ prediction confidence intervals provide a quantitative decision-support layer: narrow-CI predictions can inform therapeutic selection with high confidence, while wide-CI predictions identify patient-drug pairs for which additional experimental evidence is warranted prior to clinical action, aligning with the principles of responsible AI deployment in oncology.

\textbf{Comparison with related work}: Karathanasis et al.\ \cite{karathanasis2024machine} achieved mean $r_{pc} = 0.36$ with ElasticNet on 122 BeatAML drugs; their linear framework lacks the non-linear feature interaction capacity of PREDICT-AML. MDREAM \cite{trac2023prediction} restricts evaluation to drugs with $>100$ patient-drug pairs, excludes the pharmacological long tail, and provides no uncertainty quantification. QPOP \cite{sahib2024combinatorial} requires ex~vivo drug combination experiments that preclude scalable in silico deployment. MSDRP \cite{zhao2023msdrp} relies on cell line rather than patient-derived sensitivity data, limiting direct clinical applicability.

NetAML \cite{wang2025netaml} is a network-driven framework that trains $87$ drug-specific models on BeatAML RNA-seq data, using protein-protein interaction modules as features, and reports a mean per-drug Pearson correlation of $0.61$ across its held-out test set. Despite this promising headline figure, PREDICT-AML offers five structural advantages. (i)~\textit{Drug generalizability:} NetAML trains one independent model per compound and requires $\geq\!35$ patient samples per drug, precluding inference for novel therapeutics; PREDICT-AML's single unified model covers all $150$ BeatAML test drugs including $14$ entirely absent from training. (ii)~\textit{Multi-omics breadth:} NetAML relies exclusively on RNA-seq, omitting somatic mutations, pathway enrichment, cell-state enrichment, and patient-specific drug-pathway proximity - modalities whose combined contribution PREDICT-AML's ablation study explicitly quantifies. (iii)~\textit{Drug molecular encoding:} PREDICT-AML incorporates four SMILES-derived molecular drug representations, enabling it to leverage chemical structure for generalization; NetAML uses no drug structural encoding whatsoever. (iv)~\textit{Evaluation rigour:} NetAML employs random 70/30 splits per drug, permitting patient-drug pair leakage identical in kind to the random CV inflation ($\sim\!13\%$ in $r_{pc}$) we characterize and discourage; PREDICT-AML is evaluated on a temporally separate held-out cohort (Waves~3+4). (v)~\textit{External validation breadth and performance:} PREDICT-AML is validated simultaneously on two independent cohorts - LeeAML and FIMM-AML - achieving $|r_{pc}| = 0.557$ on FIMM-AML ($187$ patients, $78$ drugs, DSS metric) with a single model trained on BeatAML only, compared with NetAML's mean $r_{pc} = 0.38$ on the smaller FPMTB cohort ($164$ FLT3-mutant patients, $47$ drugs). PREDICT-AML thus advances the state-of-art through broad model benchmarking, simultaneous multi-cohort external validation, multi-omics and drug-structure-aware representations, and honest patient-stratified evaluation across all $150$ BeatAML test drugs.

\textbf{Limitations}: The BeatAML training cohort, while one of the largest publicly available AML pharmacogenomics resource, may not fully represent the global demographic and geographic diversity of AML patients. Drug-stratified CV performance ($r_{pc} \approx 0.37$) indicates that generalization to structurally novel compounds remains an open challenge. The FIMM-AML cross-platform evaluation is inherently constrained by systematic differences between AUC and DSS sensitivity metrics. PREDICT-AML currently predicts drug response as a continuous variable; translation to binary clinical response endpoints will require calibration against prospective clinical outcome data. Importantly, the SHAP-identified features such as %\textit{DEFA1} and 
\textit{POU4F1} act as pharmacological sensitivity modulators that currently lack direct experimental validation in the AML drug response literature and represent priority target for prospective follow-up studies. Future directions include the incorporation of single-cell transcriptomic data to resolve intra-tumoral heterogeneity, integration of longitudinal samples to capture evolving drug sensitivity, and application of causal inference frameworks to strengthen the biological interpretability of model predictions.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec5}
PREDICT-AML is a multi-omics driven AI framework for personalized AML drug response prediction that generalizes robustly to unseen patients and independent external cohorts. The optimal configuration - TabPFN with knowledge-distilled drug embeddings - achieves $r_{pc} = 0.690$ on the held-out BeatAML test set and $r_{sc} = 0.685$, outperforming all baselines and surpassing the best published ElasticNet ($r_{pc} = 0.360$) and MDREAM ensemble ($r_{sc} = 0.680$) approaches. Validation on LeeAML ($r_{pc} = 0.597$) and FIMM-AML ($|r_{pc}| = 0.557$) establishes cross-platform and cross-institutional generalizability. A key methodological finding is that standard random cross-validation overestimates performance by $\sim\!13\%$ relative to patient-stratified evaluation, which we establish as the clinically appropriate benchmark. A novel ablation result further shows that somatic mutations combined with cell-state enrichment scores alone recapitulate full multi-omics performance, offering a practical reduced-panel alternative for resource-limited settings.

SHAP analysis uncovers a biologically coherent two-tier architecture in which drug molecular identity drives population-level predictions while patient-derived features provide interpretable individual modulation, independently recovering established mechanisms such as IDH2/BCL-2 synthetic lethality and identifying POU4F1 transcriptional state as novel candidate sensitivity biomarkers without precedent in the AML drug response literature. Top-10 drug feature analysis further identifies monocyte-like cell-state enrichment, \textit{BCL6} expression, and ICD pathway activity as recurrent multi-omics determinants of predicted response, with \textit{BCL6} significant across all ten top drugs and ICD pathway significance representing a novel transcriptional biomarker axis in AML. Cross-cohort heterogeneity analysis designates Venetoclax and Panobinostat as the highest-priority agents for model-guided patient stratification. Together, these results position PREDICT-AML as an interpretable, externally validated precision oncology tool with direct potential to guide individualized therapeutic suggestion in AML.
